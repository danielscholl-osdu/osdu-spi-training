Verdict

The core idea is sound, and one decision in it is genuinely good: a single architectureMap() renders both chapter 01 and all five moments of chapter 02, at identical coordinates. The learner pays for the spatial model once, then spends everything else on the new variable. That is the thing worth protecting.

The risk is that the site was built from your question — "what is a stack?" — rather than from the reader's, which is "where is the OSDU I already run, in this picture?" The words partition, entitlements, indexer and schema appear zero times across all five files. The audience's existing knowledge is the one asset the premise depends on, and the site never spends it.

You asked four questions. Short answers: the sequence is right but has no exit and no clock; chapter 01 does not yet give a reason to explore, because its default selection is invisible; the component details do not reward curiosity, for a reason that has nothing to do with interaction; and the visual density is fine — it's the information density that is off.

The one thing

Authoring rule

Every detail panel earns its place by carrying at least one fact you could only know from having run this.

This is the only finding here that is a filter rather than a backlog item. Applied, it collapses a dozen separate "add this fact" recommendations into one test each sentence has to pass, it costs no new UI, and it makes the next forty panels better instead of repairing four of the current twenty-nine.

The proof it works is already in your file. One sentence in 1,230 words passes it:

A successful CLI exit can precede application readiness.component-details.js · rollout

That is the sentence every single review pass singled out, unprompted. It is the only one that names a failure mode the reader will actually hit.

Compare the doc this chapter conceptually mirrors. docs/design/deployment-lifecycle.md is 1,513 words — shorter than the site's panel copy — and carries 60 backticked artifacts, an eight-row stage table, and real numbers: 45–50 minutes total, ~30 for AKS, a ten-minute source timeout, a 150-minute deadline on the schema-load Job alone.

That last one matters. A learner who follows chapter 02 to the end, runs spi status --watch, and sits at a pending schema-load Job for two hours has no way to know that's normal. The site taught them the shape and withheld the scale.

You already wrote the rule

"Point at the thing: file paths, resource names, chart keys, a kubectl command… Keep numbers exact where one exists. Never round into vagueness."

osdu-spi-stack/docs/STYLE.md governs ADRs, design docs and architecture.md. It bans hedge adverbs, requires a named artifact or an exact number behind every claim, and its review checklist rejects a page that "states the same idea more than once in different forms" or "ends with a summary, a maxim, or a call to action."

The training site is written in the opposite register from the system it teaches. Not all of STYLE.md transfers — second person is right for a learner, and a takeaway is a legitimate teaching device where a maxim in an ADR is not. But point at the thing and keep numbers exact transfer completely, and applying just those two would resolve most of what follows.

Themes

THEME 01

The bridge to what the reader already knows is missing

The premise is "you know OSDU, start from there." The copy never once starts from there. This is the highest-leverage block of work on the list.

  • Partitions are absent. Zero hits. Meanwhile the repo: "Each partition gets a Cosmos DB SQL account, Service Bus namespace, and Storage account. The environment shares Gremlin, common Storage, Key Vault, and in-cluster middleware. The first partition is the primary partition and hosts the system database used by schema loading." This is the single cheapest bridge available — an OSDU engineer knows data partitions cold, and one sentence converts an abstract Azure box diagram into their existing model. Split the backend tiles into "Per partition · opendes" and "Shared by the environment."
  • Namespaces are absent, and the map has no node for "operators." The rollout panel says Flux reconciles "operators, middleware, OSDU services and initialization in dependency order" at the exact moment the map renders Flux, a gateway, a service pair and one middleware tile — the word has no visual referent. Meanwhile the CLI literally prints kubectl get pods -n foundation as a next step after spi up, so the reader's first contact with those names is an unexplained terminal table. Cheapest fix: put foundation, platform, osdu into the existing flux/middleware/service copy and add one operators tile. Then the payoff line: only osdu gets Istio sidecars; platform is deliberately outside the mesh because istio-init needs NET_ADMIN and Deployment Safeguards rejects it.
  • Chapter 03 defines SPI from zero, to an audience that has it. "SPI means Service Provider Interface…" is the third of four views — the reader's most alert attention — spent on something they brought with them. This is the best value-per-character change on the site: open from their knowledge and pivot. "You already know this boundary — it's the same provider/ split you read in every community service on GitLab. What changed is the tree around it."
  • The motivation for the entire engineering system is missing. ADR-038: upstream plans to remove its Azure implementations; provider/<svc>-azure and testing/<svc>-test-azure will be deleted and the Azure code becomes yours to own. Without that, "seeds Azure provider and test source" reads as a convenience. With it, four scattered mechanisms become one story. Worth knowing: chapter 03's own linked source, concepts.md, is stale against ADR-038 — its diagram still shows the provider arriving from upstream. Your copy is more current than the doc you send people to.
  • Profiles are never mentioned, and the payoff line is a perfect caption for your own diagram: "Profiles select Kubernetes workloads, not a smaller Azure resource estate." That maps one-to-one onto the map's AKS-box / Azure-column split. One sentence; skip the toggle.
  • The one thing the reader most needs and cannot infer: "This is not a production configuration. OSDU services share a managed identity and middleware credentials. Backup, disaster recovery, and per-service Azure access isolation are not provided by this stack." That's architecture.md line 12. Grep the site: production 0, backup 0, disaster 0. For an audience that runs production OSDU, this is a fact they will otherwise carry into a design review.
THEME 02

The arc has no exit and no clock

  • spi down appears zero times. The site walks the reader to billable Azure resources, warns them once in caption text, and offers no way out — while the repo README bolds "Remove the environment when it is no longer needed." Add a sixth moment and render it as the reverse of stage 0: boundary outline stays, workloads clear, one node remains — "Managed identities · name suffix." The footprint that doesn't go away is the lesson, and the survival behavior is genuinely surprising.
  • Duration is absent, and duration is the shape of this sequence. Moment 2 is two-thirds of the wall clock and all five render as equal-weight clicks. Add an elapsed figure to each moment in the timing pill that already exists. Do not make the step bar proportional — your own README says the moments are explanatory stages, and moment 4 explicitly overlaps moment 3.
  • spi connect is absent. The site teaches only the path of the person who creates an environment. A newly onboarded engineer is handed one. That's also why the billable warning stings: you taught the expensive path first.
  • spi token is absent, and the arc's final beat is "then make an authenticated OSDU API request." The gateway panel explains that the sidecar validates a bearer without ever saying how to get one. The command exists, composes (TOKEN=$(spi token)), and ships three identities including --no-access for 401 tests. Leaving it out reproduces the exact failure mode the engineering system exists to fix — "The tests are in the repository; the knowledge of how to run them is not."
  • The lifecycle is a chapter you already have the machinery for. ADR-029 defines five verbs with costs: status (seconds), refresh (5–20 min), upgrade (20–60 min), reset (3–6 h), teardown (15–45 min). Same map, same step-reveal component, real numbers, and it answers the question every operator asks second.
THEME 03

Every chapter header promises "Select a component to look closer." Four things quietly break that promise — which matters, because this is precisely the interaction you're trying to validate.

  • The detail panel is below the fold on the two chapters that need it. Measured at 1440×1000: the diagram is 871px tall, and the panel sits at y=1216 — 216px past the bottom of a maximized window, before any browser chrome. A reader clicks a node near the top of the map and sees nothing change. On a phone chapter 02 is 3,381px tall with the step buttons at the top and the explanation at the bottom. Chapters 03 and 04 fit fine, so it's invisible unless you check. Fix: make the panel sticky to the bottom of the figure, or move it beside the map above 1200px.
  • The first screen's default selection is invisible. selected:'environment' is a scope button whose entire selected state is a heading color shift.   • Chapter 02 fires the panel unasked, and echoes the paragraph above it. Moment 4 puts "This overlaps the final CLI work" and "Flux reconciliation overlaps the final CLI work" on screen ~400px apart, verbatim. The reader concludes the panel repeats rather than deepens, and stops clicking.

THEME 05

Structure: the panel is the wrong vehicle for ownership

A specific instance worth separating out. architecture.md presents system boundaries as a four-row ownership table — CLI and Bicep, Flux, Kubernetes controllers, Operator — each with a responsibility and a boundary, including the two best lines in the corpus: "Flux does not reconcile Azure infrastructure" and "A successful CLI exit is not an API-readiness check."

The site has that content, scattered across eleven click-to-reveal panels. For a senior engineer that is a downgrade: you converted random access into sequential discovery, and removed the ability to compare two rows. The panel is the right vehicle for "what is this box?" It is the wrong vehicle for "who owns what?"

Keep the map and the click-to-explain. Add the four-row table below it. Different question, different instrument.

The same logic points at what the missing chapter should be. The Flux dependency graph is 17 Kustomizations with named dependsOn edges, and the repo's own advice is "A downstream error may name only its direct dependency. Follow that dependency upstream rather than assuming the last blocked service is the root cause." That's the thing a reader will need at 2am, it's inherently a graph, and it's the one diagram that can't be a static picture.

THEME 06

Nothing below a chapter is linkable

#bring-up is the deepest shareable address, and it always opens on whatever moment the module-level variable happens to hold. Verified: leave chapter 02 at moment 5 and come back, and you land on "Replay from the start ↺" directly beneath an intro that says "Move through five moments." A reload resets; the back button does nothing.

The most common real use of a training artifact is one engineer pasting a colleague a link to one specific picture. Parse #chapter/step, make the controls navigate rather than mutate, and gate the scroll reset on the chapter key actually changing. Roughly a dozen lines, and it deletes the stale-state bug as a side effect.
